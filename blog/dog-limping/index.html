My Dog Is Limping — Should I Go to the Vet?

5 min read · Updated May 2026

It's one of the most common — and most stressful — things a dog owner experiences. Your dog is limping. Maybe it came on suddenly after a walk, or maybe you woke up and noticed it this morning. Now you're staring at them, trying to decide: is this serious? Do I need to go to the emergency vet right now? Or can it wait until the morning?

The honest answer is: it depends. Limping in dogs has a huge range of causes — from a tiny thorn in the paw pad to a broken bone to life-threatening conditions like bone cancer or a torn ligament. The key is knowing which signs point to an emergency and which ones are safe to monitor.

Here's how to assess your dog's limping in under 5 minutes.

Step 1 — Is your dog putting any weight on the leg?

The very first thing to check is whether your dog is bearing any weight on the affected leg at all. This single factor tells you a lot.

  • Bearing some weight: Your dog is putting at least a little weight on the leg. This suggests the injury is likely less severe — a sprain, mild strain, or minor soft tissue injury.
  • Not bearing any weight (non-weight-bearing): Your dog is completely off the leg, hopping on three legs, or holding it up entirely. This is a more urgent sign and warrants a same-day vet visit at minimum.

Step 2 — Check the paw first

Before assuming the worst, always check the paw. Dogs pick up thorns, glass, burrs, and small stones regularly — especially after walks. Gently examine each paw pad, between the toes, and around the nail beds. Look for:

  • Cuts, scrapes, or puncture wounds on the pads
  • A foreign object lodged in the paw or between toes
  • Swelling or redness between the toes
  • A cracked or broken nail — these are surprisingly painful
  • Burns from hot pavement (common in summer)

If you find a small thorn or splinter and can remove it easily and safely, do so. The limping will often stop almost immediately. If you find a deep puncture wound, significant bleeding, or something you can't safely remove, that's a vet visit.

Step 3 — Look for swelling, heat, or deformity

Gently run your hands along the leg from the paw upward. Compare it to the opposite leg. You're looking for:

  • Swelling or puffiness at any point along the leg or joint
  • Heat — the leg or joint feels noticeably warmer than the other side
  • An obvious deformity — the leg sits at an unusual angle or has an unnatural bend
  • A painful reaction when you touch a specific spot

Any of these findings alongside limping warrants a vet visit — ideally same-day.

The urgency guide — what to do based on what you see

✅ Watch at home — monitor closely

  • Mild limping, still bearing weight on the leg
  • No swelling, heat, or deformity
  • No wound or foreign object found
  • Dog is alert, eating, and otherwise acting normally
  • Limping started after exercise or rough play

⚠️ See your vet within 24 hours

  • Non-weight-bearing but no obvious deformity
  • Mild swelling at a joint
  • Limping that hasn't improved after 24 hours of rest
  • A cut or wound on the paw that needs attention
  • Your dog is licking or chewing at the leg excessively

🚨 Go to the emergency vet now

  • Obvious deformity — the leg looks broken or is at an unnatural angle
  • Bone is visible through the skin
  • Significant, uncontrolled bleeding
  • Your dog cries out in pain when the leg is touched or moved
  • Sudden non-weight-bearing after a fall, jump, or collision
  • Your dog collapses or cannot stand at all
  • Limping combined with pale gums, rapid breathing, or obvious distress

Does breed matter?

Yes — significantly. Some breeds are far more prone to specific conditions that cause limping:

  • Large breeds (Labs, Golden Retrievers, German Shepherds): Higher risk of hip dysplasia, cruciate ligament tears, and bone cancer — all serious conditions that cause limping.
  • Small breeds (Chihuahuas, Pomeranians, Yorkshire Terriers): Prone to luxating patellas (kneecap dislocation), which causes an intermittent skipping or hopping gait.
  • Dachshunds: At higher risk of intervertebral disc disease which can cause sudden limping or even paralysis.
  • Older dogs of any breed: Arthritis is extremely common and typically shows as a gradual worsening limp, especially in the morning or after rest.

When in doubt, use the 2-hour rule

If your dog starts limping and you're unsure, rest them for 2 hours. No walks, no stairs, no jumping. If the limping significantly improves or resolves with rest, it's likely a minor soft tissue strain — monitor closely and book a regular vet appointment if it doesn't fully resolve within 24-48 hours.

If the limping stays the same or gets worse after 2 hours of rest, that's your signal to call your vet.

What will the vet do?

If you do visit the vet for limping, they'll typically start with a physical examination — watching your dog walk, palpating the joints and muscles, and checking range of motion. Depending on what they find, they may recommend X-rays to check for fractures, joint issues, or bone abnormalities. Treatment varies widely depending on the cause — from simple rest and anti-inflammatories to surgery for torn ligaments or fractures.

The bottom line

Limping in dogs is never something to completely ignore, but it's also not always a reason to panic. A calm, systematic assessment — paw check, weight bearing, swelling and heat — will tell you most of what you need to know. When in doubt, rest your dog and monitor closely. If things don't improve or get worse, call your vet.
